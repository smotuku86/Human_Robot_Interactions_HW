Strategy 1 was for Robot 1 to always go for whatever it thought Robot 2 was least likely to go for. Cube selection was based on running a 
distance-based probability calculation and choosing the cube with the smallest probability. Robot 1 scored 40/100 and Robot 2 scored 65/100. 
This shows that always playing aggressively does not necessarily result in the highest score. I observed several collisions, and almost all 
of them resulted in at least one (or both) of the robots being unable to score. These cases usually occurred because Robot 2 chose a random 
cube near Robot 1, and they collided on their first step toward it.

Strategy 2 builds on Strategy 1 but aims for a safer style of play. Two new features were added to the control system.
The first was a check on cube locations. If the most likely and least likely cubes for Robot 2 are near each other, 
it is reasonable to assume that all the cubes are clustered closely together. In this case, it is better for Robot 1 
to sit the round out and allow Robot 2 to attempt to score. The second feature allowed Robot 1 to observe Robot 2’s 
first move before acting. This was implemented to maintain competitive play while minimizing collisions in the first step.
By observing during the first step, Robot 1 can also better predict Robot 2’s intended goal. This led to Robot 1 scoring 
61/100 and Robot 2 scoring 74/100. This was a significant improvement for Robot 1. Collisions occurred far less frequently, 
although some edge cases still remained (aside from occasional failures to pick up a cube). The two main edge cases observed 
were situations where Robot 1 would change its goal at the last second or be unable to reach far enough to grasp a cube.

These two strategies show us that employing safety into a control policy that orginally had no safety can improve scores.
